Featured note / First-Gen Voices

What I Learned Making First-Gen Voices

I started a podcast because first-generation students were already trading useful stories privately. These are the lessons I am taking from making 15 episodes.

Why I made it I wanted a thoughtful place for first-generation student stories without trying to speak for every first-generation student.
At a glance 15 episodes
2,000+ downloads
Started in 2024

I started First-Gen Voices in 2024 because I kept hearing first-generation students trade useful stories privately. The official information on campus often sounds certain. Real experience usually has a lot more uncertainty in it.

I wanted to make a thoughtful place for those stories. I was not trying to speak for every first-generation student. I wanted to make room for one person's experience at a time, and to listen closely enough that the conversation stayed useful.

I am the founder and producer, so I handle the recording, editing, cover art, and release scheduling. The working titles changed a lot along the way, and I still add notes whenever I remember something.

Ask fewer questions, then listen

For the first episode, The First Week Nobody Explains, I had 14 questions in the outline. I kept looking down at the page instead of listening. The part I liked most happened after I stopped following the outline.

For Episode 2, Asking for Help Before You Are Stuck, I used a shorter outline. The question “What made asking for help easier?” worked better than trying to plan every turn of the conversation.

In Episode 3, Office Hours Are for You Too, I learned that silence feels longer while recording than it does when listening back. I jumped in too fast. In Episode 11, Learning to Say I Belong Here, I had to remind myself to wait after asking a question and not answer it myself.

I talk more when I am nervous. A short follow-up is usually better: “Can you tell me more about that?”

Treat each story as one story

Episode 4, When Family Does Not Speak College, made me more careful about making one person's experience sound universal. I started saying in the introduction that this is one person's experience. When I listened back, I looked for questions where I had assumed too much.

Before Episode 9, Money Conversations We Avoid, I sent the topic outline ahead of time so there would be no surprises. I also learned that a conversation does not need every personal detail to be useful.

Specific is more useful than inspirational

In Episode 5, The Hidden Cost of Commuting, I delayed the release because I kept changing tiny edits. Clear and respectful is better than perfect.

In Episode 10, Internships and the Experience Gap, the plain introduction was better than one that tried to sound more impressive. In Episode 14, Graduation Advice That Actually Helped, I kept the specific advice instead of making the ending inspirational. Specific is more useful.

A process matters more than motivation

My production checklist is not complicated:

  • check the audio levels
  • remove obvious distractions
  • listen through
  • export
  • make the artwork
  • schedule the release

For Episode 6, Working While Studying, I used the checklist and did not have to remember every step. For Episode 12, Transferring and Starting Over, I had a busy week, but the same checklist helped me release the episode anyway.

Let listener questions shape what comes next

After Episode 7, Finding Community Without a Map, a listener asked where someone should start if they do not know anyone. I saved the question because it could become another episode.

By Episode 13, What I Wish Faculty Understood, several follow-up questions were about talking with professors. I added that theme to my future list. For Episode 15, The Questions We Keep Getting, I used the listener-question list while planning. Questions are not just feedback after publishing; they can help decide what to explore next.

If you want to try this yourself

  • Prepare a few questions, not a script for every moment.
  • Ask one short follow-up, then wait.
  • Describe the conversation as one person's experience.
  • For a difficult topic, send the main topic and questions before recording.
  • Use a production checklist from recording through scheduling.
  • Save recurring listener questions for future ideas.

What I am taking forward

The notes keep pointing me back to fewer questions and more listening, consistency over perfection, and specific advice instead of polished inspiration.

I also keep reminding myself not to say I am “giving people a voice.” People already have voices. My job is to listen well, ask a useful follow-up, and make a thoughtful place for what they choose to say.

I am still figuring out what would actually help another student creator most, but I know the questions and the process are worth keeping.

Thanks for reading. I hope one of these small lessons helps you start.
