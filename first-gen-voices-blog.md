# What I Learned Making *First-Gen Voices*

I started *First-Gen Voices* in 2024 because I kept hearing first-generation students trade useful stories privately. The official information on campus often sounds certain. Real experience usually has a lot more uncertainty in it.

I wanted to make a thoughtful place for those stories. I was not trying to speak for every first-generation student. I wanted to make room for one person's experience at a time, and to listen closely enough that the conversation stayed useful.

I am the founder and producer, so I handle the recording, editing, cover art, and release scheduling. The podcast has reached 15 episodes and more than 2,000 downloads. The working titles changed a lot along the way, and I still add notes whenever I remember something. Looking back at those notes has made the biggest lessons easier to see.

The lessons fall into five parts: listen more, treat each story as one story, choose clear and specific work over perfection, build a process, and let listener questions guide what comes next.

## Ask fewer questions, then listen

For the first episode, *The First Week Nobody Explains*, I had 14 questions in the outline. I kept looking down at the page instead of listening. The part I liked most happened after I stopped following the outline.

That changed how I prepare. For Episode 2, *Asking for Help Before You Are Stuck*, I used a shorter outline. The question “What made asking for help easier?” worked better than trying to plan every turn of the conversation.

I am still practicing this. In Episode 3, *Office Hours Are for You Too*, I learned that silence feels longer while recording than it does when listening back. I jumped in too fast. In Episode 11, *Learning to Say I Belong Here*, I had to remind myself to wait after asking a question and not answer it myself.

I also know that I talk more when I am nervous. A short follow-up is usually better than filling the space: “Can you tell me more about that?”

## Treat each story as one story

Episode 4, *When Family Does Not Speak College*, made me more careful about making one person's experience sound universal. I started saying in the introduction that this is one person's experience. When I listened back, I looked for questions where I had assumed too much.

I am still thinking about whether to send the main topic and questions before recording. For difficult subjects, I did find that preparation helped. Before Episode 9, *Money Conversations We Avoid*, I sent the topic outline ahead of time so there would be no surprises.

That episode also taught me that a conversation does not need every personal detail to be useful. The goal is not to collect everything someone has lived through. It is to make room for what they actually want to share.

## Specific is more useful than inspirational

In Episode 5, *The Hidden Cost of Commuting*, I delayed the release because I kept changing tiny edits. The reminder I wrote down was simple: clear and respectful is better than perfect.

That lesson came back in Episode 10, *Internships and the Experience Gap*. I almost rewrote the introduction to make it sound more impressive. The plain introduction was better. I do not want to turn every person into a success story.

In Episode 14, *Graduation Advice That Actually Helped*, I caught myself trying to make the ending inspirational. I kept the specific advice instead. Specific is more useful.

## A process matters more than motivation

My production checklist is not complicated:

- check the audio levels
- remove obvious distractions
- listen through
- export
- make the artwork
- schedule the release

For Episode 6, *Working While Studying*, I used the checklist and did not have to remember every step. For Episode 12, *Transferring and Starting Over*, I had a busy week, but the same checklist helped me release the episode anyway.

That made me think that consistency might be more about having a process than feeling motivated. I do not need every week to feel easy. I need a way to keep moving through the work.

## If you want to try this yourself

Here is the starting point I would take from these episodes:

- Prepare a few questions, not a script for every moment.
- Ask one short follow-up, such as “Can you tell me more about that?”, then wait.
- Describe the conversation as one person's experience instead of making it sound universal.
- For a difficult topic, send the main topic and questions before recording so there are no surprises.
- Use a production checklist: audio levels, obvious distractions, a full listen-through, export, artwork, and scheduling.
- Save recurring listener questions and use them when planning what to explore next.

## Let listener questions shape what comes next

After Episode 7, *Finding Community Without a Map*, a listener asked where someone should start if they do not know anyone. I saved the question because it could become another episode.

Episode 8, *Choosing a Major Without a Blueprint*, brought more practical questions. People want to hear the story and what the person tried next. I am considering a final interview question for future episodes: “What first step would you suggest?”

By Episode 13, *What I Wish Faculty Understood*, several follow-up questions were about talking with professors. I added that theme to my future list. The list is becoming useful because it shows which questions keep coming up.

For Episode 15, *The Questions We Keep Getting*, I used the listener-question list while planning. Questions are not just feedback after publishing. They can help decide what to explore next.

## What I am taking forward

The notes keep pointing me back to the same things:

- fewer questions, more listening
- consistency over perfection
- listener questions becoming future episode ideas
- care around the difference between one person's story and a universal claim
- specific advice instead of polished inspiration

I also keep reminding myself not to say I am “giving people a voice.” People already have voices. My job is to listen well, ask a useful follow-up, and make a thoughtful place for what they choose to say.

Creating *First-Gen Voices* has taught me that the work is both the conversation and the process around it. Prepare enough to make someone comfortable. Listen enough to notice what you did not expect. Finish the episode instead of endlessly perfecting it. Then pay attention to the questions that remain.

That is what I have learned so far from making 15 episodes. I am still figuring out what would actually help another student creator most, but I know the questions and the process are worth keeping.
